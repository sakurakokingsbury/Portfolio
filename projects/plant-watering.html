Featured Project

Smart Watering System

Designed and built a compact IoT-enabled indoor plant watering system that monitors soil moisture, air humidity, temperature, and illuminance, then automatically actuates a servo-controlled valve to water the plant when needed.

Project Overview

This project focused on designing, manufacturing, and deploying an automated watering system for small indoor potted plants. The final product combined a custom 3D-printed mechanical structure, embedded sensors, servo-controlled water delivery mechanism, and IoT data publishing to a website accessible on a smartphone or computer.

The system was designed to monitor four environmental conditions: soil moisture, atmospheric temperature, air humidity, and illuminance. These readings were collected through sensors and published to the Stevens IoT network at 10-second intervals, allowing plant conditions and system performance to be tracked over multiple days.

A major focus of the project was balancing product design, mechanical packaging, electronics placement, water exposure, 3D-printing time limits, and reliable automated performance. The final system successfully watered the plant when soil moisture dropped below the selected threshold and collected multiple days of data.

Mechanical Design

The mechanical design was developed as a compact 3D-printed attachment rather than a full replacement pot. This allowed the product to clamp onto an existing indoor plant pot while supporting the water reservoir, valve, servo motor, sensors, and wiring in one integrated structure.

The first prototype had dimensional issues and did not fully account for the water container. The design was revised to fit the plant pot more securely, hold the water supply upright, and organize the electronic components toward the rear of the system to reduce exposure to water.

The final design used a clamp-on geometry that kept the device compact while still allowing the soil sensor to reach the plant, the valve to control water release, and the electronics to remain accessible during testing and debugging.

One important mechanical challenge was the valve actuation. The valve had to be partially disassembled and adjusted so the servo motor could rotate it without excessive resistance. This required the mechanical design and servo calibration to be developed together rather than treated as separate parts of the system.

Design Iteration

The project followed an iterative design process that moved from hand sketches to CAD modeling, 3D printing, component fitting, electronics integration, and final deployment. Each revision was driven by physical testing and the need to satisfy real constraints such as pot size, print time, component packaging, water exposure, and system reliability.

Initial Concepts

Each team member created a concept sketch exploring different ways to mount the device, hold the water supply, position the sensors, and package the electronics around the plant.

Concept Selection

The selected design was chosen because it best met the project constraints. It was more compact than a full-pot design and provided a better foundation for holding the required hardware within the allotted print time.

Prototype Redesign

After the first print, the CAD model was revised to correct component-fit issues and add space for the water container. The clamp geometry and component layout were adjusted to better match the actual hardware dimensions.

Valve Integration

The valve and servo motor required additional tuning because the original valve resistance was too high for reliable actuation. The valve was modified and the servo angles were determined experimentally through trial-and-error testing.

Final Deployment

The final assembly integrated the printed structure, sensors, servo-actuated valve, water supply, wiring, and IoT publishing code into a working system that successfully collected multi-day plant data.

Electronics and System Architecture

  • ESP32-based microcontroller for sensor reading, servo control, and IoT communication
  • Soil moisture sensor for determining when the plant required water
  • DHT sensor for atmospheric temperature and air humidity monitoring
  • Photocell sensor for measuring plant light exposure through illuminance
  • Servo motor for opening and closing the mechanical water valve
  • MQTT publishing system for sending sensor readings to the Stevens IoT network

The system architecture centered on a microcontroller that collected environmental data, evaluated the soil moisture level, and actuated the watering valve when needed. The device used sensor feedback rather than a fixed watering schedule, allowing the system to respond to the actual condition of the plant.

Electrical integration was especially important because the system combined water storage, exposed wiring, sensors, and a moving valve mechanism. The final mechanical layout kept the electronics positioned away from the main water path while still allowing access for debugging, rewiring, and deployment testing.

Software and Control Logic

The software collected readings from the soil moisture sensor, DHT sensor, and photocell. Raw analog readings from the soil moisture sensor were mapped into a percentage so the system could compare the current soil condition against a watering threshold. The photocell reading was converted into an illuminance value to monitor light exposure.

The watering behavior was controlled by a threshold-based algorithm. When the soil moisture percentage dropped below the selected limit, the servo motor opened the valve, held it open briefly to release water, then returned to the closed position. The final open and close angles were determined through physical testing because the correct values depended on the printed geometry, servo placement, and valve resistance.

The system also published temperature, air humidity, soil humidity, and illuminance readings to MQTT topics on the Stevens IoT network every 10 seconds. This allowed the team to verify that the sensors were working during deployment and collect the required multi-day dataset.

A major software issue occurred when the device appeared to collect sensor readings locally but failed to publish data online. Debugging showed that the MQTT connection was not being maintained during the loop. Adding the missing mqtt_loop(); call resolved the issue and allowed the system to publish data correctly.

Challenges and Engineering Decisions

Designing Around Physical Components

The first printed prototype revealed that the CAD model did not fully accommodate all hardware, especially the water container. The final design required more accurate component packaging and a layout that considered the actual dimensions of the bottle, valve, servo, sensors, and wiring.

Water and Electronics Placement

Because the product combined water storage with exposed electronics, the internal layout had to reduce the risk of water contacting the wiring or microcontroller. The final design placed electronics toward the back of the device and separated them from the primary water flow path.

Servo and Valve Actuation

The valve created too much resistance for the weak servo motor in its original condition. To make the mechanism functional, the valve was partially disassembled and adjusted, then the servo angles were tuned through repeated tests until the valve opened and closed reliably.

Sensor Debugging

The soil moisture sensor worked correctly in an isolated test sketch but produced unreliable readings in the main program. The code had to be reorganized and rebuilt around the working sensor logic so the full system could correctly read soil humidity during deployment.

IoT Publishing Failure

During deployment, the system collected sensor data locally but did not publish it to the IoT server. The issue was traced to a missing MQTT loop call, which prevented the network connection from updating properly. Once fixed, the device published data correctly.

Balancing Function and Manufacturability

The design had to remain compact, printable, and visually clean while still holding all required components. The final print time stayed well below the six-hour limit, which helped meet the manufacturability constraint while preserving functionality.

Design Performance

The final system met the major design requirements by fitting onto the plant pot, holding the water supply, measuring four environmental variables, actuating the valve when watering was needed, and publishing sensor data to the IoT network. The device successfully collected data over multiple days after the final deployment fix.

The collected data showed realistic indoor plant behavior. Temperature readings remained relatively stable because the room environment did not change dramatically, while illuminance varied more noticeably with light exposure. Soil moisture and humidity readings changed in response to watering behavior and surrounding conditions.

The final product demonstrated a complete closed-loop watering system: the sensors measured plant conditions, the code interpreted the data, the servo-controlled valve responded to dry soil, and the IoT system recorded performance over time.

Final Engineering Takeaways

This project provided early experience designing a complete electromechanical product where the mechanical structure, sensor layout, wiring, software, and physical actuation all depended on one another. Small mechanical decisions, such as valve placement and electronics location, directly affected the reliability of the software-controlled watering behavior.

The project also strengthened my ability to design around real hardware constraints rather than ideal CAD geometry. Component tolerances, wiring access, water exposure, servo strength, and print limitations all had to be considered before the system could function as a complete product.

Most importantly, this project showed me how iterative testing connects different engineering disciplines. Debugging the final system required moving between SolidWorks, 3D printing, circuit assembly, Arduino code, MQTT publishing, and physical deployment until the design worked reliably as a full integrated system.
